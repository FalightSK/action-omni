\begin{abstract}
Does robot adaptation improve a frozen vision--language backbone when its
original action expert is replaced? We compare stock \qwen{} with its
robot-adapted descendant \groot{} using matched downstream heads. In the
submitted ALOHA evaluation, the paper reported 61.25\% versus 51.75\% over two
runs per backbone. A new matched fixed-endpoint study over three independent
training seeds found means of 49.3\% $\pm$ 6.0 and 39.8\% $\pm$ 11.0,
respectively, with a mean paired difference of 9.5 points favoring \groot{}.
The direction reversed for one seed pair, so three seeds do not establish a
stable training-population effect. In each pair, \groot{} had lower offline
validation loss, yet \qwen{} won the seed-3 rollout comparison; validation loss
did not consistently identify the closed-loop winner. The revised ALOHA recipe
and evaluation cohort differ from the submitted protocol, and the new runs were
not instrumented for gripper behavior. Historical gripper traces describe an
observed pattern, not a proven cause. Claims remain limited to these frozen
backbones and the tested ALOHA setting.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Vision--language--action policies connect visual and language representations to
robot actions. OpenVLA, $\pi_0$, and GR00T N1 illustrate approaches to learning
generalist policies from robot demonstrations~\cite{openvla,pi0,grootn1}.
Released systems such as \groot{} combine a robot-adapted backbone with their
own action experts~\cite{groot17}. Component studies examine which backbone,
architecture, and data choices matter~\cite{robovlms}. Comparing complete systems
does not isolate the usefulness of a frozen backbone when a downstream user
learns a new controller. Our study examines that narrower deployment choice.

We compare \qwen{} with the vision--language subtree extracted from \groot{}.
The released lineage includes Cosmos-Reason2-2B between these endpoints
\cite{groot17}. Accordingly, an endpoint difference includes more than
the final robot-training stage. Tensor differences establish that checkpoint
weights differ, but do not identify which training data or adaptation step
caused a downstream difference. An optional intermediate-checkpoint experiment
is planned, not reported as completed.

Two questions organize the study. \textbf{RQ1:} how does the frozen endpoint
contrast vary with camera configuration and benchmark, and how reliable is it
across independent head training? \textbf{RQ2:} do the selected offline measures
reflect the observed closed-loop comparisons? ALOHA transfer-cube provides a
fixed-instruction bimanual handover~\cite{aloha}; LIBERO-Goal provides
ten instruction-conditioned single-arm tasks~\cite{libero}. Their differences make
them informative evaluation settings, but prevent attribution of a cross-benchmark
contrast to one property such as arm count or action space.

The submitted study evaluated six configurations in 1,600 primary rollouts,
with checkpoint ladders and 400 instrumented ALOHA rollouts in support. Its
contributions are the comparison of camera and endpoint contrasts on a common
success-rate scale, a staged description of handover failures, and a concrete
offline--online mismatch. We retain that evidence and organization here, while
correcting its scope. Separate batches of scenes evaluate existing policies;
they do not measure variation from initializing and training another policy.
The revised protocol tests reliability without assuming that the original
ordering will persist.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{VLA design studies.} RoboVLMs studies backbone, architecture, and data
choices across more than 600 experiments~\cite{robovlms}; OpenVLA, $\pi_0$, and
GR00T N1 study integrated robot policies~\cite{openvla,pi0,grootn1}. Our smaller
component study removes the released action expert and compares frozen features
using a shared downstream recipe within each benchmark. It does not evaluate
the complete GR00T policy or establish interactions across a broad model family.

\textbf{Observation configuration.} Hsu et al. study how hand-centric views
affect learning and generalization~\cite{handview}. We examine a wrist-camera
control in a frozen-VLM setting and report its success-rate contrast alongside
the endpoint contrast. This makes the magnitudes comparable within LIBERO;
it does not isolate observability as the cause of all residual failures.

\textbf{Evaluation validity.} Critical Interval MSE studies offline validation
focused on task-critical segments~\cite{cimse}. We examine selected aggregate
losses and diagnostics, without testing that method or claiming that all offline
evaluators fail. LIBERO-PRO reports sensitivity to evaluation conditions and
instruction perturbations~\cite{liberopro}. Our language ablation includes a
fixed-instruction ALOHA control to distinguish task-discriminative information
from sensitivity to removing text tokens.

\section{Experimental Design}
\subsection{Frozen endpoints and downstream heads}
Both endpoints are read at language layer 16. This is the retained final layer
for the extracted GR00T backbone and an intermediate read for the stock model.
The latter receives its language stack's final RMS normalization at that read.
This controls the specific final-versus-intermediate normalization discrepancy;
it does not make the released systems identical or isolate a single pretraining
dataset. Processor settings, view order, token padding, and read depth are
matched within each benchmark and recorded with the feature cache.
In the revised path, processor-masked padding is removed and real tokens are
right-padded with zeros to a fixed budget, avoiding batch-dependent pad features.

The backbone is frozen and its image/text features are cached. Only a token
adapter and a DiT-style~\cite{dit} flow-matching~\cite{flow} action head train.
The adapter supplies conditioning
and tokens to a transformer decoder. Given demonstrated actions $a$, Gaussian
noise $\epsilon$, and $t\sim\mathcal U(0,1)$, the loss is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal L=\mathbb E\left[\left\|v_\theta((1-t)\epsilon+ta,t,c)
                    -(a-\epsilon)\right\|_2^2\right].
\end{equation}
The new heads are matched between endpoints within each benchmark. They are
\emph{not} identical across benchmarks: the inspected configurations contain
19,165,319 trainable parameters for two-camera LIBERO and 14,014,990 for ALOHA.
Both predict 16 actions and execute eight before replanning, with three
integration steps at inference. The configurations use decoder widths 320 and
256, respectively. These differences limit cross-benchmark interpretation.

\subsection{Demonstrations and validation}
The revised protocol holds out whole demonstration episodes. A deterministic
split with seed 42 assigns ten percent of episodes from each LIBERO task to
validation, with at least one held-out episode per task. For ALOHA's 50
demonstrations, 45 train and five validate. All frames and action chunks from
an episode remain in that split; chunks are padded only within their episode.
The same split is shared across backbones, training seeds, and camera controls.

ALOHA state and absolute joint targets use mean/std statistics computed from
training episodes only. The same statistics are used offline and in the simulator,
with predicted actions denormalized before execution. LIBERO retains its raw
end-effector delta actions and recorded state representation. Validation loss
measures demonstrated action prediction on held-out episodes; test success
measures the policy's behavior under its own actions in simulation. The two
quantities are not interchangeable.

\subsection{Training and checkpoint selection}
The prospective ALOHA comparison trains three independent heads per backbone,
with seeds 3, 4, and 5 controlling initialization, shuffling, dropout, and flow
noise. Both endpoints use the same episode split and training-only normalization.
The locked recipe uses AdamW, weight decay $10^{-2}$, a warmup--cosine schedule
with peak learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$ for the decoder and half that rate for
the adapter, batch size 256, and 100 epochs. The 300-update warmup completes
before patience is counted; the minimum and maximum are both 100 epochs, so the
predeclared fixed-final checkpoint is epoch 100. This differs from the submitted
training budget and is a prospective matched comparison, not an exact
reproduction. No checkpoint or hyperparameter is selected using test success.
Cache metadata and checkpoint signatures link the split, normalization,
encoder settings, code, and training seed; mismatched records are rejected.

\subsection{Closed-loop evaluation}
Each ALOHA policy is evaluated on the same 200 scene IDs (10000--10199); within
each training seed, both backbones share initial states, environment seeds, and
policy-sampling seeds. The records preserve these seeds separately, allowing
paired episode tests conditional on the trained policy pair. The evaluation uses
\texttt{render\_samples=0} and saves no video or trace; this rendering change from the
submitted setting is another reason the new rates are not an exact reproduction.

The primary ALOHA evaluation is uninstrumented; trace capture is opt-in for a
separately named diagnostic run. Trace mode records actions, pre- and post-step
states, reward, and available per-replan attention. For seed 0, the matched
50-start trace screen scored 6/50 versus 5/50 untraced, so instrumentation
noninterference is not established. Diagnostic counts must reconcile with the
success records. LIBERO
demonstration replay and ALOHA action-plumbing checks examine different aspects
of the harness. Neither is proof that the policy has reached a performance
ceiling. Numerical harness-validation results require rerunning their checks.

\subsection{Statistics}
Report each policy's successes and attempts, then the unweighted mean and sample
standard deviation across the three independently trained heads per backbone.
Per-policy Wilson intervals describe rollout uncertainty conditional on a
trained policy; the across-head SD describes variation across only three seeds.
Historical run-level summaries are kept separate from this prospective estimate.

For each endpoint pair and training seed, show the paired $2\times2$ outcome
table, the success-rate difference, and an exact two-sided McNemar test.
Paired percentile-bootstrap intervals use 20,000 resamples. These intervals do
not cover unseen tasks or population-level training variability. The locked
family contains six primary tests; for ALOHA we report the Holm adjustment across
its three completed seed pairs and a Bonferroni adjustment for the full family.
Three seeds provide a limited estimate of training variability; episode-level
significance cannot establish a general training-population effect.

\section{Closed-Loop Comparisons}
\subsection{Evidence status}
The submitted ALOHA paper reports two independent runs per backbone, each on a
disjoint 200-start rollout cohort. The original checkpoints, training logs, and
per-episode outcomes were not recovered, so their training provenance and full
paired analysis cannot be independently audited. The new prospective ALOHA
comparison includes three verified training seeds per backbone. Historical and
new results are reported separately because their training and evaluation
protocols differ.

\subsection{Camera configuration and LIBERO-Goal}

camera increased submitted success by 29.0 points for GR00T and 21.0 points for
stock Qwen. These contrasts were larger than the 2.5-point endpoint contrast
under two cameras. A large camera contrast does not establish statistical power
for a smaller backbone difference. Moreover, removing a view changes the input
configuration relative to robot pretraining; the one-camera rows are observation
controls rather than a clean measure of the robot-training stage alone.

\subsection{Observed receiving-gripper failures}
Instrumented ALOHA rollouts recorded states, commands, staged rewards, and
per-replan attention on a separate 200-start cohort per endpoint.
Table~\ref{tab:failures} preserves the original behavioral categories, with
counts and explicit post-lift denominators. The receiver never opened in

reasoning. Historical classification used endpoint-specific opening thresholds

no commanded or observed gripper traces and cannot validate this mechanism.
The final category is renamed operationally: a gripper that opens
and closes without completing transfer need not have ``closed on nothing.''

The trace cohort's success rates (60.5\% and 51.5\%) differ from the original
first-batch rates (60.0\% and 49.0\%). Logging noninterference is therefore not
established by similar aggregate rates. Likewise, the failure-category contrast
cannot be subtracted from the pooled 400-start conditional gap to assert that
one category explains the whole difference: the cohorts and denominators differ.

The submitted attention summaries were lower for stock in both successful and
unsuccessful episodes. Attention is correlational, and replans within an episode
are dependent. Expert-demonstration probes also predict the opening event from
proprioception; they do not identify the cue used by the deployed policy, but
preclude an exclusively visual explanation from these observations alone.
The revised implementation supports separately named, opt-in traces, calibrates
a common threshold from training episodes, checks threshold sensitivity,
distinguishes commands from observed opening, and averages post-lift attention
within episodes. The seed-0 screen produced 6/50 successes with tracing and 5/50
without tracing on matched starts; this does not establish systematic
instrumentation noninterference. The new matched evaluation above was untraced;
all of its unsuccessful rollouts are recorded as 400-step truncations, which
does not identify a gripper-specific failure cause.

\subsection{Harness checks and bounds on interpretation}
Submitted LIBERO demonstration replay succeeded in 45/50 episodes without
settling and 46/50 with five settling steps. Replay is a useful action-plumbing
check, but is neither a policy ceiling nor proof of overall harness validity.
Under swapped instructions the submitted LIBERO configurations scored 0/200;
this supports instruction dependence under that intervention, without proving
robust semantic understanding or excluding all memorization.

The benchmarks differ in instruction variation, arm count, degrees of freedom,
action space, camera configuration, and relation to robot-pretraining data.
Downstream head size and training schedule also differ. Bimanual coordination
and distribution match are possible explanations for the endpoint contrast;
this design cannot separate them. The optional Cosmos ALOHA control would
separate an intermediate adaptation from the final stage within this lineage,
but is not completed and would not establish cross-lineage generalization.

\section{Offline Metrics and Closed-Loop Behavior}
\subsection{ALOHA mismatch and LIBERO ranking reversal}
\textbf{Answer to RQ2 for ALOHA: offline validation loss did not consistently
identify the closed-loop winner.} The submitted diagnostics in
Table~\ref{tab:offline} differed only slightly alongside the historical
9.5-point success contrast. In the new matched study, GR00T had lower best and
final validation loss than Qwen3-VL in all three seed pairs, but Qwen3-VL won the
seed-3 rollout comparison. The validation-loss minimum occurred at epochs
16--21, while the fixed epoch-100 checkpoint had higher loss for every policy.
Final training losses were 0.039--0.041 and final validation losses were
0.266--0.284. This gap is consistent with late-training overfit but does not
establish why closed-loop performance differs. The new test used a predeclared
fixed-final checkpoint; selecting the validation-best checkpoint after seeing
test results would change the protocol.

LIBERO shows a configuration-ranking reversal (Table~\ref{tab:libero}). The
two-camera configurations had the largest reported validation loss (0.0352)
and the highest success (91.5\% and 89.0\%). Selecting the lowest-loss row
would instead choose the 62.5\%-success configuration. This comparison changes
camera input as well as the learned policy; it is not a pure backbone contrast.
Expert-state validation may miss the value of a wrist view in states reached
during rollout. The earlier frame-based validation split also limits what can
be inferred about generalization from these losses.

\subsection{Why aggregate diagnostics can be insufficient}
A receiving-gripper deviation occupies one of fourteen action dimensions and
only part of a trajectory. Averaging across dimensions, chunks, and phases can
dilute its contribution even when it matters for task completion. That is a
plausible explanation of the observed mismatch, not a mathematical guarantee
that mean squared error cannot detect the failure. Task-critical validation,
such as the approach investigated by Critical Interval MSE~\cite{cimse}, remains
an alternative to evaluate rather than a method invalidated by this study.

The original discussion drew an analogy to language-model emergence, where
smooth changes in an upstream measure need not imply a smooth downstream
capability curve~\cite{emergent}. We retain this as a conceptual caution only:
our two-endpoint study does not test scaling or identify an emergence mechanism.
Neither similar attention nor feature similarity is a calibrated success
predictor. LIBERO's endpoint comparison rests on rollout outcomes, not on an
assertion that offline agreement proves equivalent policy performance.

\subsection{Revised offline validation}
The six matched ALOHA records show a direct offline--online mismatch: GR00T's
best and final validation loss is lower in every seed pair, but it does not win
all three paired rollout comparisons. Only five whole episodes are held out for
validation, so these losses have limited episode-level coverage. Across policies,
the validation minimum is reached at epochs 16--21 and rises by the fixed final
checkpoint. This supports the narrow conclusion that validation loss did not
reliably rank these tested ALOHA policies; it does not prove that validation
loss is generally uninformative.

\section{Limitations}
All endpoint evidence comes from one lineage, simulated tasks, and a
frozen-backbone readout. Intermediate adaptation and robot training are combined
in the endpoint contrast. The submitted ALOHA paper reports two independent
training runs per backbone, but their checkpoints and per-episode records were
not recovered for independent audit. The revised matched ALOHA study has three
training seeds per backbone; its sample SD is still imprecise, and its protocol
differs from the submitted experiment.

The head, conditioning path, one fixed demonstration split, and benchmark-specific
recipes restrict generalization. Cross-benchmark contrasts do not isolate
embodiment or task complexity. Nonsignificance is not equivalence, and conditional
rollout intervals do not cover unseen policies or tasks. Stage rates, attention,
and failure categories are descriptive; no causal intervention such as activation
patching was run. Full-backbone fine-tuning, other model lineages, more diverse
tasks, and physical robots remain outside the present evidence. The revised
protocol and optional intermediate control do not remove those limits.

\section{Conclusion}
The submitted ALOHA result reported a 9.5-point advantage for GR00T across two
reported independent runs. In the new matched fixed-endpoint ALOHA study, the
three-seed means were 49.3\% $\pm$ 6.0 for GR00T and 39.8\% $\pm$ 11.0 for
Qwen3-VL, also a 9.5-point difference. The effect varied substantially by seed
and reversed once, so this small replication does not establish a stable
training-population advantage. Validation loss favored GR00T for all three
pairs but did not identify the closed-loop winner consistently. The new
evaluation was uninstrumented; historical gripper-opening behavior remains an
observed failure pattern, not a demonstrated cause. The result is specific to
these frozen backbones, training recipe, and simulated ALOHA task.
